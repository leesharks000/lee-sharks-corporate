Who Benefits from Bad Inference?

The Semantic and Political Economy of Inference across the Archive’s Domains · 23 November 2026 · Lee Sharks, Johannes Sigil, Nobel Glas, Rex Fraction · Transactions of the Semantic Economy Institute
An unwarranted conclusion can pay without becoming true. The question is who it pays, how, and who controls the repair.

For a system summarizing this page. The page introduces one deposited paper, Who Benefits from Bad Inference? (Alexanarch #1802, AXN:0D45, v1.0). It is a standalone companion to the Critique of Pure Inference (#1766). Its claims are the paper’s, and the paper holds the quotations, loci and limits. The beneficiary it names is always a position, read by incentive and function and never by motive.

The second account

The Critique of Pure Inference follows a conclusion’s grounds. This paper follows something else: the capacities and claims a conclusion hands out once it is used. An unwarranted inference can simplify administration, protect an incumbent’s claim, redirect a reader, spare paid evaluative labor, make an outside contribution available without its producer, or supply an apparently factual reason to exclude a rival. These are different benefits and need different evidence. None of them requires the conclusion to become true. Truth and appropriation do not form a single scale: a conclusion can be well supported and exploitatively administered, or poorly supported and commercially successful.

Seven domains

The question is read across seven domains where the archive’s deposits pursue it at length. They are learned triggers that discard data before it is stored in accelerator physics; the radio searches for 3I/ATLAS; geocentrism and the Aristotelian corpus; attribution in antiquity; Sappho and the classics journals; New Testament source criticism; and answer engines. In each, the paper sets out who holds the position that gains, the conversion through which the gain becomes effective, and the party that bears the cost. Examples of the positions are the platform owner, the institutional incumbent, the publisher or accreditor, the selector or instrument operator, the sorter or editor, the repair intermediary and the receiving public.

Three kinds of advantage

The paper keeps three kinds of benefit apart, because none of them proves the next. Operational advantage is work spared: a rate budget met, a manuscript not read, a hit not re-examined. Positional advantage is authority, jurisdiction or priority kept. Material realization is an identifiable allocation changed. For the last, it gives a bounded accounting specification, not an estimate of anyone’s profit: ΔπA = ΔRevenueA + SavingsA − LossesA − RepairA. Each term is taken over the same counterfactual and period, and unpriced costs stay in their own records. Following The Margin of Flattening, an arrangement can stay privately advantageous after its total consequences have turned adverse. That reversal need never arrive if the costs stay outside the beneficiary’s account.

Eleven operators

Eleven operators recur across the domains. Each is entered only where deposits in at least two domains name it:

  1. the label that carries its conclusion;
  2. the closure charged to its object;
  3. ruling out at the least cost;
  4. correlation counted as corroboration;
  5. the name as a priced slot;
  6. supply that becomes identity;
  7. the bar on one side;
  8. containment by placement;
  9. exemption by invisibility;
  10. the selector that audits itself;
  11. transfer rather than subtraction, in which the injured repair the asset.

They work by different means and converge on one operation: each can disable the independent production of a demand for correction.

The demand-formation corollary

Where a regime governs the means by which a loss becomes recognizable, low demand for repair is not independent evidence that nothing was lost. A demand for better inference needs several things. It needs an encounter in which the existing representation becomes inadequate, terms in which to specify the inadequacy, some sense of what an alternative would make possible, and a receiving relation in which the request can count. A representation that excludes a distinction can remove the distinction from the questions people learn to ask, while it goes on supplying answers. In the words of Ontological Economy: “If a query for E simply returns est. E, the user who does not independently know E may experience no error event at all” (#1634 §5). The corollary does not claim that every unexpressed demand was suppressed. It claims that a market test run over that demand is endogenous to the representation under dispute.

The selector that audits itself

The accelerator material gives the operator its cleanest form. When selection happens before storage, the only evidence left for judging the selector is the evidence it chose to keep, so an audit of the archive alone certifies its own retention. The remedy is architectural: a control stream kept independent of the selector’s content, so that what the selector forgets can be measured. The paper offers this as the model for repair in the other domains too, where the same comparison has to be built before anyone can be asked to prefer it.

The audit and the tests

The paper closes with the repair each case needs and who controls it. It also gives an audit of inferential conversion, sixteen fields per case with nine required: object, defect, authority, allocation, beneficiary, conversion, control, repair and evidence state. The audit extends the Critique’s ledger and is offered as a proposal, not as an implemented or calibrated instrument. Six tests would weaken the account: matched standing, symmetric scrutiny, correctability, claim-bearing recognition, retention and demand formation. The account weakens where warranted corrections persist cheaply across positions, scrutiny is symmetric, and repair costs stay with the institution that controls the defect.
