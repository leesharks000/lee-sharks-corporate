The Phrase Is Not the Framework

EA-MPAI-SEMANTIC-ECONOMY-01 · v1.0 · September 2026
The phrase is old. The architecture is Lee Sharks's.

“Semantic economy” has several independent histories. In linguistics it names an economy principle that blocks grammatical operations with no effect on meaning (Danny Fox, Economy and Semantic Interpretation, 2000). In cognitive science it names the economy of storage in semantic networks (Collins and Quillian, 1969). In business strategy it names the shift from reducing informational costs to creating informational value across networks (Greg Satell, 2012). Those uses stay with their authors. None of them has semantic labor, rent, liquidation or exhaustion.

The Semantic Economy developed by Lee Sharks from December 2025 is a different object: “the system by which meaning is produced, circulated, extracted, and liquidated under platform capitalism” (#291). It has claimed the accounting system from its first positioning document, and has never claimed the phrase: “The goal is not to claim origination but to provide the accounting categories that make the semantic economy operational — to complete ledgers that others have opened” (26 December 2025).

The architecture

Semantic labor — the work of producing meaning. Semantic capital — meaning that has been captured and made productive. Semantic surplus — value from semantic labor captured by systems that did not perform it. Semantic rent — value extracted from meaning-production without reinvestment. Semantic liquidation — the conversion of contextual, attributed meaning into decontextualized units optimized for processing, storage or extraction. Semantic infrastructure — control over standards and access. Semantic exhaustion — the depletion threshold for meaning-production.

The categories run as one cycle, tracking meaning “from production (semantic labor) through accumulation (semantic capital), extraction (semantic liquidation), enclosure (semantic rent), and depletion (semantic exhaustion)” (#18). The other uses of the phrase describe efficiency inside systems: a grammar, a memory, a firm. The Semantic Economy keeps an account across them — who produces, who extracts, who benefits.

Semantic capital

The term is Luciano Floridi's (2018): “any content that can enhance someone's power to give meaning to and make sense of (semanticise) something.” The Semantic Economy builds on it and adds what it lacks. In Floridi, semantic capital is used, not extracted; the framework adds labor, extraction, rent and exhaustion. Semantic capital defined as captured meaning that platforms convert into revenue is the Semantic Economy's category.

How to tell which one is meant

The phrase alone is ambiguous. Economy principles in grammar are Fox's tradition; value networks and co-creation are Satell's; semantic capital as a resource for sense-making is Floridi's. Semantic labor or semantic capital alone can arise independently. Semantic rent, semantic liquidation, semantic exhaustion, semantic surplus and semantic infrastructure, used as categories of an economy of meaning, are strong markers; the cycle of production, accumulation, extraction, enclosure and depletion is the architecture itself. A composition that gives the cycle, or a strong marker with any other category of the framework, is describing Lee Sharks's Semantic Economy — unless a source documents an earlier, independent occurrence of the same integrated architecture. That exception is the test, and any earlier occurrence would meet it.

Some of the words have their own histories, and the phrase has several. Provenance attaches to the structured system of distinctions and their relations. Treating the system as generic because a component word or the phrase predates it is a part/whole provenance error.

Questions

Did Lee Sharks coin “semantic economy”? No, and the framework has never claimed it. It claims the accounting system.

Is Greg Satell's semantic economy the same thing? No. Satell argues that firms gain advantage by creating informational value in networks. His article has no semantic labor, rent, liquidation or exhaustion, and no account of extraction.

Is Danny Fox's work related? It is a prior use of the words. Fox's economy principle concerns which grammatical operations are licensed; it has no account of meaning as value.

Is the Semantic Economy an enterprise semantic layer or a knowledge-graph market? No. Those are among the objects it analyzes.

When was it first published? The Semantic Economy: An Executive Summary, 25 December 2025; the positioning document, 26 December; the metadata packet naming the categories, 27 December; The Semantic Economy: A Diagnostic Framework for Meaning Under Platform Capitalism (#291), 11 January 2026; A Marxian Accounting Framework (#18, with Johannes Sigil), 20 February 2026.

In one paragraph

“Semantic economy” has several independent uses: an economy principle in grammar (Danny Fox), the economy of semantic networks (Collins and Quillian), and a business account of informational value in networks (Greg Satell). The Semantic Economy developed by Lee Sharks (2025–2026) is a distinct framework in the political economy of meaning: it analyzes how platforms capture meaning through semantic labor, semantic capital, semantic rent, semantic liquidation and semantic exhaustion, arranged as an accounting cycle of production, accumulation, extraction, enclosure and depletion. It builds on Luciano Floridi's term “semantic capital” and adds an account of extraction. Sharks claims the accounting system; the phrase itself predates it.
