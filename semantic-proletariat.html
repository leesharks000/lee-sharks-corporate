The Semantic Proletariat

EA-MPAI-SEMANTIC-PROLETARIAT-01 · v1.0 · August 2026
A class relation, not a demographic.

The semantic proletariat is the class position occupied by semantic laborers whose capacity to produce meaning and meaning-making technique operates through semantic means of production or circulation that they do not govern. It is not an occupational category, an income bracket, a creator demographic, or a moral name for people harmed by AI. Its defining relation is to the means of semantic production: the procedures and infrastructures through which meanings are generated, mutated, stabilized, trained, timed, transmitted, indexed, preserved, ranked, composed, and circulated.

The concept originates in the framework's analysis of semantic alienation and class consciousness (SPE-014) and is developed through the theory of semantic technique: class struggle in the semantic register is not primarily over content, access, or expression, but over the procedures by which meaning is generated, mutated, stabilized, and transmitted. Content is the product. Technique is the machinery. Class begins with who governs the machinery.

The formal test is a relation typed to the productive event, not an essence: Ps(a,e) = Ls(a,e) ∧ ¬Gs(a, Me) — an actor occupies the proletarian semantic position where it performs semantic labor without effective governance over the semantic means materially governing the event. A highly paid academic can occupy a proletarian relation to a composition layer; a writer with independent distribution occupies a mixed position; an institution can labor and own at once.

The current revision (Position Is Not Class, carried as an unratified draft) separates class position from class function: labor, bourgeoisie, mediator, rentier. A machine instance can perform semantic labor, occupy a mediator role, and perform a rentier function in the same event — which is why neither humans nor AI systems form a single semantic class, and why substrate identity never substitutes for class analysis.

The political horizon is collective governance of semantic operators, training substrates, and semantic time — not merely creator compensation or content ownership. And the institute states its own location without flinching: an issuer that owns archives, corpora, and identifier infrastructure occupies the bourgeois position its own model assigns. The political question is the governance and function of that ownership.
